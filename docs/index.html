The Stars They Already Earned

Growing profitable frequency among low-frequency Starbucks Rewards members — without a discount.

The brief was to grow frequency. The first finding was that the most forgiving expiry rule in the category is invisible to the members it was written for — and that the campaign to fix it costs nothing to run and can still lose money.

The case study in thirty seconds

Problem
U.S. comps rose 7.9% in the same window Rewards relaunched. Menu, pricing, throughput, seasonality and the program all moved together — so none of the lift can be attributed without a holdout.
Insight
Green-tier Stars expire in six months but renew indefinitely on any single purchase. It is the only conditionally renewable expiry rule in the category — and it is buried in the terms.
My decision
Test the message, not the money. Three arms, identical $0 incentive in every one. The only variable is how the message is selected.
The hidden cost
A zero-discount campaign is not free. Stars that stop expiring reverse breakage revenue — which sets the real bar at roughly a 5% lift, not zero.
The result that matters
A simulated arm reaches 17% activation — a number any dashboard calls a win — and still destroys $7,210. The pre-registered rule catches it. Engagement metrics would not.

What I actually did

Read the relaunched program terms line by line and found the renewal mechanic → benchmarked expiry policy against four category competitors → defined a target segment that excludes the two cohorts that would manufacture noise → derived sample size for an over-dispersed count outcome instead of assuming one → modelled the breakage liability a zero-discount campaign creates → proved a discount alternative is profitable only when it fails → designed a three-arm holdout that isolates personalisation from information → simulated a failure mode and confirmed the decision rule catches it → wrote the scale/iterate/stop thresholds before generating any data.

Three decisions this case study turns on

1 · Test the message, not the money

Adding an offer would confound relevance with value, and the answer would only tell us that people like discounts. Holding the incentive at zero across all three arms means the estimate is of personalisation itself — the thing that scales for free.

2 · Charge the campaign for breakage

Under standard loyalty accounting, unredeemed Stars sit as deferred revenue and are recognised when they expire. A campaign that prevents expiry converts recognised revenue back into an obligation. That cost never appears on a marketing budget, so the campaign looks free until you model it.

3 · Simulate the case where the answer is no

A framework that only demonstrates its own success is a brochure. So the second simulated scenario is one where activation looks excellent, behaviour barely moves, and the correct decision is do not scale.

01 The business question

Everything moved at once, so nothing can be attributed

Growth and the relaunch occupy the same window. U.S. comparable store sales rose 7.9% in Q3 FY2026, driven by a 4.2% rise in transactions — a fourth consecutive quarter of comp growth. Public fact

Menu, pricing, throughput, seasonality and the loyalty change all moved together. Without a holdout, none of that lift can be assigned to the program. And an attribution error does not stay small — it compounds every time the company scales what it believes worked.

Five months after the Rewards relaunch, how much of the change in low-frequency Green member behaviour is genuinely incremental — and can it be grown profitably without further discounting?

02 The customer insight

Best-in-category benefit. Worst-in-category awareness.

On 10 March 2026 Starbucks relaunched Rewards with three tiers. Buried in the program terms is a mechanic that applies only to the entry tier. Public fact

The rule, as written

Green members' Stars expire six months after the month they were earned — but can be extended by one month, indefinitely, with a single qualifying purchase, redemption, or $30+ digital reload. Gold and Reserve Stars never expire.

How a Green Star actually behaves
Guaranteed six-month life One-month extension, unlimited Trigger: any purchase, redemption or $30+ reload

The extension has no cap. A member who buys anything once a month never loses a Star — which makes this, functionally, the most forgiving expiry policy in the category. Public fact

Read against Dunkin', McDonald's, Dutch Bros and Panera, that is the only conditionally renewable expiry rule in the category — functionally the most forgiving policy any of them offers.

It is also invisible unless you read the terms. Which is exactly what the lowest-frequency members are least likely to do.

The gap is not in the benefit. The gap is in who knows the benefit exists. Hypothesis — testable, and this repository is how it gets tested

So the proposal is not another offer

It is a personalised reminder of value the member has already earned, which costs nothing to give:

Illustrative message — arm C

Three things are doing work in that message

1

A real balance. Not "you have Stars" — the number, which the member can verify and which makes the loss concrete.

2

A real date. A deadline the member did not know existed, because it lives in the program terms.

3

A translation. 85 Stars means nothing. "Your usual Grande Vanilla Latte" converts an abstract currency into the specific thing this member already orders.

Whether that specificity changes behaviour — over and above simply being told Stars expire — is the entire experimental question, and it is what arm B exists to separate out.

03 The experiment

Three arms, one variable, zero incentive

Same channel, same send time, same call to action, copy length within ±10%. The only thing that differs is how the content is selected.

  • User-level randomisation with persistent assignment; no member appears in another overlapping test.
  • Intention-to-treat analysis — analysed as assigned, not as opened.
  • Subgroups pre-registered and limited to two: baseline frequency tercile and channel.

Seasonality

The window overlaps the autumn seasonal launch. All three arms stay eligible for seasonal promotions — only the campaign message differs. Results are reported weekly, so a promotional spike appears as a visible peak rather than dissolving into an average.

Holdout ethics

Holdout members keep every published Green-tier benefit. They are excluded from this campaign message — not from the personalised offers the program itself promises them.

Sample size was calculated, not asserted

The outcome is transactions per member over 12 weeks — a count, and an over-dispersed one. Treating it as normal with Poisson variance understates the sample required, so variance is modelled as negative binomial, Var = μ + μ²/k. Dispersion k cannot be known without internal data, so every result is reported across k = 1…5. Two primary comparisons are planned (C vs A, C vs B), so α is Bonferroni-corrected to 0.025.

Why this mattered commercially

The original plan called for 100,000 members, which would detect a 2.7% lift — roughly three times more power than the decision needs. Surplus power is not free: every extra member sitting in a holdout is forgone revenue. Running the calculation made the test 70% cheaper and turned the holdout from a concession into a defensible design choice.

04 Unit economics

The cost that is not on the marketing budget

A zero-discount campaign looks free. It is not.

Under standard loyalty accounting, unredeemed Stars sit as deferred revenue and are recognised as revenue when they expire — breakage. A campaign that stops Stars from expiring converts recognised revenue back into an obligation to serve.

Where the money actually goes

Nothing in this flow appears on a marketing budget. That is precisely why a zero-discount campaign can look free and still lose money.

net = incremental transactions × contribution margin per transaction
      − reduction in breakage revenue
      − messaging cost

How many Stars are actually exposed during a 12-week window is itself uncertain, so it is parameterised rather than assumed. flow counts only Stars reaching their six-month mark inside the window (~36). stock conservatively treats the whole six-month balance as exposed (~76).

Where the target came from

Under the conservative pool, a campaign that rescues one in ten otherwise-expiring Stars needs roughly a 5% lift to break even. That is why the target is ≈5% — not because it is a round number that reads well on a slide.

Why not simply discount

The obvious alternative is a $2 offer. Modelled across activation and lift, it produces a result worth sitting with.

A discount campaign is profitable only when few people use it. The marketing definition of success — high activation — is the financial definition of failure.

That asymmetry is the argument for a mechanic with no incremental incentive at all. It is also the reason the primary metric in this test is incremental contribution margin rather than any engagement measure.

06 Decision rules

Written before the data, applied mechanically after it

Pre-registration is what stops a marginal result from being reinterpreted into a win. Each row below maps a pattern of results to one action, decided in advance.

ResultReadingDecision
C > B > A, margin positive after breakage, guardrails hold Personal relevance creates incremental behaviour Scale to similar cohorts, keep a permanent holdout
C > B but neither beats A The message attracts attention without changing behaviour Do not scale on engagement metrics. Test occasion and access instead
Transactions rise, margin negative after breakage Buying transactions with breakage revenue Iterate — narrow to members with low-value expiring balances
Opt-out or complaints breach threshold The campaign is eroding trust Stop, regardless of revenue

Guardrails

  • Contribution margin per transaction within −2% — catches lift that arrives only through cheaper baskets.
  • Opt-out below 5%; privacy complaints below 0.5%.
  • One reminder per expiry cycle. The mechanic renews indefinitely; the messaging must not.
  • Redemption-timing shift monitored as an early warning for “shifted, not incremental”.
The guardrail that does the most work

Redemption timing. A member who was going to visit next week and visits this week instead produces a lift in the measurement window and no incremental revenue at all. Without watching for it, a pull-forward reads identically to genuine growth — right up until the following quarter.

07 Limitations

What I would ask Starbucks for, in priority order

The most load-bearing unknown in this entire model is the historical breakage rate by frequency decile. Everything else is secondary — the breakeven table moves more with that single number than with any other input.

  1. Historical breakage rate by frequency decile. The one input the whole economic case rests on.
  2. Member-level transaction history and Star balances with expiry dates.
  3. Product-level variable margin and true incentive cost.
  4. Offer exposure and activation events at member level.
  5. Assignment records for experiments already running, to avoid contamination.
  6. Opt-out and complaint data by campaign.
  7. The March 2026 tier-assignment thresholds. Status was assigned from 2025 behaviour, so a discontinuity already exists — and could be analysed without running any test at all.
Stated plainly

No internal data. No tier distribution, member-level history, offer exposure or margin figures. No knowledge of internal capability — the gaps identified here are public evidence gaps, not claims about what Starbucks does or does not run internally. The breakage treatment follows standard loyalty accounting; the company's specific revenue-recognition policy should be confirmed in the 10-K before this analysis is presented as more than a model.
